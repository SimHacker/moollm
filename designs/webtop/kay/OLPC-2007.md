# The OLPC threads, 2007: Kay on SimCity, black boxes, and languages for children

Seven discussions, archived by Don in his own repo at
`micropolis/turbogears/micropolis/htdocs/static/html/alankay.html`, each mirroring a
`donhopkins.com` node. Participants across the set include **Alan Kay, SJ Klein, Guido van Rossum,
Vanessa Freudenberg and Don**, on the OLPC lists across **March and November 2007** — the March
discussion on `sugar@laptop.org` about languages for children, and the November one occasioned by
Don's plan to reimplement SimCity for the XO.

This is the substantive "discussions with Alan Kay" material. What follows is extraction with the
argument attached — the analysis of where it lands is in [README.md](README.md).

**Three notes on the archive itself.** The mirror's page title reads *"Discussions with Alan Key"* —
a typo for Kay. Three identical copies of the mirror exist in Don's trees, all 320 lines, all
carrying the same seven nodes — 132, 134, 135, 137, 139, 140, 145 — so none of them repairs the
others. The node 140 transcription **dropped text** in several places, and those passages are
restored below from the list archive.

**The dating in the mirror is also misleading.** Node 140 and node 137 are from **March 2007**, on
`sugar@laptop.org`, not November; the November thread is the SimCity and Robot Odyssey one at node
145. The "black boxes," "gist-view," and *"SimCity is similar but more pernicious"* passages are all
in the March discussion.

And the third note matters most, because it changes what the archive says. **The mirror is an
excerpt, and the excerpting hides an argument.** Node 137 carries Kay's message and Guido van
Rossum's one-line reply — *"Thanks Alan. I'm quite satisfied with this response and I agree with the
priorities!"* — and stops there, so the exchange reads as agreement. The list archive is public,
still live, and shows a **53-message thread** in which the disagreement runs for another two weeks.
`lists.laptop.org/pipermail/sugar/2007-March/`, messages 001695–001947. That archive also holds
clean copies of the corrupted node 140 passages. The full thread is archived and read in Don's own
repo, under `characters/don-hopkins/correspondence/threads/2007-03-sugar-python-etoys-visual-programming/`.

## The one that matters most for a system built on readable files

From **node 132, Kay on programming languages** — and if you read one thing in this archive, read
this:

> "It's hard to point to any programming language for beginners that has a really great form. One
> thing that has consistently worked is **'close to natural language but clearly not natural
> language'**. That is, it really helps if the **gist-view** of a program is a kind of metaphor for
> what it does, even if one has to think harder about the detailed meaning.
>
> "For children, Hypercard was OK in many respects for the gist-view, but **was too like English for
> both deep understanding and for programming** (many children had a hard time getting past the idea
> that Hypercard couldn't understand and do any reasonable English sentence). This was debated
> endlessly in Logo circles, and Logo wound up going from a much more English-like syntax to one much
> more like Lisp (**this was a big mistake in my view**). Finding the balance between these is
> critical, because it governs **how much brain is left to the learner to think about content rather
> than form.**"

**Why this is the sharpest thing in the archive for us.** HyperTalk's failure mode was that it looked
*too much* like English, so users expected it to understand any English sentence and were
disappointed. MOOLLM writes its skills and characters as markdown and YAML read by a model — which
looks exactly like the same trap, except the failure inverts: **the system now can understand any
reasonable English sentence**, so the expectation HyperTalk couldn't meet is one an LLM largely
meets. The disappointment moves rather than disappearing: it lands on the boundary between what the
model will do reliably and what it will do once and then not again.

Kay's criterion survives the inversion intact. *Close to natural language but clearly not natural
language* is a demand for a **visible boundary**, and it is the reason YAML structure earns its keep
next to prose — the structure is the part that says *this is not conversation, this is a form with
edges.* The gist-view framing is also exactly the semantic-pyramid argument, thirty years earlier and
better named.

## Science as map-making, with annotations for error

From **node 140**, a long essay on science education. This passage is the Know Knob's actual
ancestor:

> "Scientists escape to a large extent from simple belief by having done enough real experimentation,
> modeling building using mathematics that suggests new experiments, etc., to realize that **science
> is more like map-making for real navigators than bible-making**: IOW, the maps need to be as
> accurate as possible **with annotations for errors and kinds of measurements**, done by competent
> map-makers rather than story tellers, and they are always subject to improvement and rediscovery:
> they never completely represent the territory they are trying to map."

*Annotations for errors and kinds of measurements* is the signed-assessment record with its
`evidence` dimension, stated in 2007 as a property of good maps. And *bible-making* is the word he
reuses on Interactive Physics below, which makes it a consistent position rather than a metaphor he
happened to reach for.

## Gray boxes that pop open, and the center that moves

Also node 140, and this is better than the way [SIGNED-ASSESSMENTS.md](../SIGNED-ASSESSMENTS.md) currently states its own
thesis:

> "How much should the learners/users have to do by themselves vs. how much should the
> curriculum/system do for them? […] the learner does the important parts by themselves, and any
> black or translucent boxes serve only on the side and not at the center of the learning. **What is
> the center and what is the side will shift as the learning progresses, and this has to be
> accommodated.**"

> "As the children get more sophisticated, **the black and gray boxes that scaffold what they are
> doing can be popped open** and understood and modified. For example, 'forward' (which moves an
> object in the direction of its 'heading') is a black box initially, and very useful in that form.
> But there is a point when the children will be greatly aided by understanding that forward is just
> a vector addition and is a method made from a more fundamental idea. **The underlying language for
> the system itself has to reveal itself as the same species as what the children have been
> learning.**"

Three things fall out. Opacity is **graduated and moves with the reader**, which is semantic zoom
applied to mechanism rather than to text. *Same species* is the symmetry claim — the system's own
substrate must be made of what the user was taught, which is the filesystem-and-markdown argument
and the homoiconicity argument at once. And *on the side, not at the center* is a placement rule for
abstraction: a black box is fine until it holds the thing you are supposed to be learning.

## The SimCity criticism, in three registers

**node 134** is a letter to Don, and it opens by applauding him:

> "I'm writing to **applaud you** for your plans to reimplement SimCity for children on the OLPC.
>
> "My main complaint about this game has always been the **rigidity, and sometimes stupidity, of its
> assumptions** (counter crime with more police stations) and the **opaqueness of its mechanism**
> (children can't find out what its actual assumptions are, see what they look like, or change them
> to try other systems dynamics).
>
> "So I have used SimCity as an example of an **anti-ed environment** despite all the awards it has
> won. **It's kind of an air-guitar environment.**
>
> "In the past, I tried to get Maxis to take the actual (great) educational possibilities more
> seriously, but to no avail."

Then the constructive form, which is a design brief:

> "Going to Python can help a few areas of this, but **a better abstraction for the heart of Sim-City
> would be a way to show its rules/heuristics in a readable and writable form.** […] I am suggesting
> that some deep design be done to come up with a **'behavior modification interface'** that allows
> real creativity on the part of the children. So it is **much more than stringing black boxes
> together** or having to deal with fragile procedurals."

**node 140** puts it in the harshest register, alongside Interactive Physics:

> "Two cautionary examples are Interactive Physics and SimCity. The first assumes that Newton was
> absolutely right […] The users are restricted to parameterizing the internal dynamic models and
> cannot see them, question them, or change them. […] This is most assuredly not in the spirit of
> science! **It amounts to a dynamic bible.**
>
> "SimCity is similar but **more pernicious**. It is a **black box of 'soft somewhat arbitrary
> knowledge'** that the children can't look at, question or change. For example, SC gets the players
> to discover that the way to counter rising crime is to put in more police stations. **Most
> anthropologists, sociologists, psychologists, and economists would disagree violently.** Alternate
> assumptions can't be tried, etc.
>
> "Both of these packages have won many 'educational awards' from the pop culture, but in many ways
> they are **anti-real-education** because they miss what modern knowledge and thinking and
> epistemology are all about."

And **node 139** settles the question of who he said it to:

> "**I actually argued with him [Will Wright] and Maxis for not making SimCity very educational.**
> E.g. the kids can't open the hood to see the assumptions made by SimCity (crime can be countered by
> more police stations) and try other assumptions (raise standard of living to counter crime) etc.
> I've never thought of it as a particularly good design for educational purposes."

The bracket appears in the archive as mirrored; whether Kay typed it or it was added when the page
was assembled is not determinable from the mirror, and brackets conventionally mark an editorial
insertion. What the sentence establishes either way: **the criticism was delivered to Wright and to
Maxis directly, by Kay's own account, and it did not take.** Note also the thread it arrives in —
the subject line is *Robot Odyssey*, not SimCity. Don's published reply is node 135: open it to
scripting, document and parameterize the mechanism, and build an Etoys-like visual language for
extending it — which is Micropolis.

## Robot Odyssey and Rocky's Boots, which he loves

From node 139 and node 134, since they are the counterexample that makes the SimCity criticism
specific rather than general grumbling:

> "I have exactly the opposite opinion of Robot Odyssey, which I thought was a brilliant concept when
> the TLC people brought it to me at Atari in the early 80s. (**Rocky's Boots is pretty much my all
> time favorite for a great game that really teaches and also has a terrific intro to itself done in
> itself**, etc. Warren Robinett is a very special designer.)
>
> "The big problem with Robot Odyssey […] was that the **circuits-programming didn't scale to the
> game**. They really needed to move to something like an object-oriented event-driven Logo with
> symbolic scripting […] **the bang per effort dropped off precipitously.** […] The failure of Robot
> Odyssey really pained me because I thought that the concept of this game was one of the best ever
> (still is)."

He was **Chief Scientist at Atari** at the time, and says Warren Robinett worked for him. *An intro
to itself done in itself* is the self-teaching artifact this whole cluster keeps trying to build, and
Rocky's Boots is his named favorite for it — a much more useful pointer than the SimCity complaint.

And a loose end worth chasing, from node 139, addressed to Don about the Etoys Robot Odyssey:

> "we now have funding and are really going to do it this year. **Want to help design and build it?**"

## The warning that applies to us

Node 140, on why good environments stop improving — this is aimed at Squeak, Logo, and everyone
including himself, and it is the same shape as the ambient-layer critique in
[OBJECTIONS.md](../OBJECTIONS.md):

> "One of the parasitic difficulties is that **computer environments, once made (with lots of effort
> and dedication) tend to form tribal bonds that are rather religious in nature.** The amount of
> effort required plus the attendant religion makes it extremely difficult to take new insights and
> ideas and make brand new better environments for the children. **The strong tendency is to use and
> reuse and incrementally expand the old environments.**"

And from node 137, the same point turned on tooling loyalty:

> "We live in a computing world in which there is a tremendous amount of identification between many
> programmers and the tools they use — so strong that it resembles religious fervor. From my view,
> **ALL of the systems have such flaws that we are better off being critical of all of them** and try
> to use the best ideas from everywhere."

A repo with a constitution, an ambient layer, and a house vocabulary is exactly the kind of artifact
this describes. Worth keeping somewhere it will be re-read.

## Substrate indifference, with one non-negotiable

Node 137, in the thread with Guido van Rossum:

> "Viewpoints Research (our little non-profit) doesn't have any 'ego or identity' staked around
> whether the children's authoring environment is Python based or Squeak based. […]
>
> "However, **I will personally fight to the death** to make sure that there is a children's
> authoring environment that allows even young children to do simulation style programming with very
> rich media objects. […] The base could be Javascript (if implemented on top of an integrated
> environment of sufficient power), Python (ditto), Ruby (ditto), etc. Whatever it is, it has to be
> **above high thresholds, not a hack or a gesture.**"

> "Besides the programming the children use to learn important ideas in math and science, they also
> need to be able to see how their own computer world is structured by being able to **'pop the hood'
> on practically everything they use.** […] I think there needs to be a **wrapping on the entire set
> of facilities that uses the same conventions** that 9 year olds do their own programming in."

Guido's reply: *"Thanks Alan. I'm quite satisfied with this response and I agree with the
priorities!"*

**Language is negotiable; pop-the-hood is not.** That is a useful licence and a useful constraint at
once: it says the choice of substrate is ours to make and the editability of the whole stack is not.
*One wrapping, same conventions, all the way out* is the directory-as-object claim in different
words.

## The charge that he lived inside Smalltalk, and what the record shows

There is a persistent read of Kay — Jaron Lanier put it to Don in as many words — that he lived in
his own Smalltalk world. It is worth testing against this thread rather than settling by
impression, because the thread is the hardest available case: he is being asked to concede his own
system's place on a machine that had chosen someone else's language.

**What he actually wrote, in the same message quoted above:** no ego or identity staked on Python
versus Squeak; if the base is Python then the authoring should be Python too; the base could be
Javascript, Python or Ruby; and *"ALL of the system have such flaws that we are better off being
critical of all of them and try to use the best ideas from everywhere."* He had also been saying it
in public for a year — from the June 2007 list, **"There is nothing sacred about Etoys,"** followed
by the complaint that he had *tried* to get the Python community to build an Etoys-like environment
and found no takers.

**The charge fails on the substrate and holds on the threshold.** What he would not concede is not a
language, it is a floor: *"above high thresholds, not a hack or a gesture."* That is a demanding
position and people on the receiving end of it can reasonably experience it as immovable. But
refusing to accept a gesture is not the same failure as refusing to leave your own system, and
collapsing the two is what produces the caricature.

**The more interesting evidence is one level down, from the Etoys maintainer.** Vanessa Freudenberg,
who was doing the actual work of keeping Squeak/Etoys running on the XO, answered Guido's "anathema"
by naming what really blocks popping the hood:

> "If anything is an anathema, then it's the huge body of impenetrable C code in linux, the
> libraries, X11, gecko, gtk, cairo, and, yes, underlying Python, too, **and even Squeak, though to a
> much lesser extent.**"

`sugar@laptop.org 001721, 9 March 2007.`

**Squeak is on her list.** The person with the most to lose from the comparison applied the
critical-of-all-of-them standard to her own system, in the same sentence as gtk, and then conceded
that *"most of it couldn't"* — she struck the hedge herself — *"be done in Python, but for whatever
reason, nobody does it."* And what she pointed at as VPRI's actual answer was not Squeak either: the
STEPS / *Inventing Fundamental New Computing Technologies* programme, whose whole premise was
replacing the stack Squeak sits on.

Guido conceded in the same exchange that he could not build Sugar and that a 1995-era 7MHz handheld
switched apps faster than the XO, and then named the tribalism he had run into: *"at least some of
the people who can do it are stuck in the 'Smalltalk is superior' rut."* **Both poles conceded
something real within one round.** The tribe existed; these three were not it.

## On Papert, and on his own contribution

Node 140, worth keeping for the credit discipline alone — quoted in full from the list archive, where
the passages the mirror dropped are intact:

> "A central realization was that (a) the differential geometry of vectors **(Gauss was one of the
> parents of this perspective)** fit very readily into how children thought about themselves in the
> world, and that (b) the computer could show this world graphically and also easily do the laborious
> integration of the differential equations **to give children the deep hit of fundamental powerful
> ideas of vector calculus in forms they could recognize and use for their own ends. I still regard
> these insights by Seymour as among the top few of all time regarding what computers are really good
> for.**
>
> "**My contribution to this was small**, and amounted to adding in the ideas (a) that multiple
> independent objects **(an idea derived from the early world of simulation)** which children could
> program in the manner of LOGO would amount to a world of real children's mathematics **that could
> model many kinds of ideas, including those of science (and of course all previous computer
> structures and old and new media)**, and (b) that **the entire environment including the
> math/programming languages the children used were properly part of the user interface** and had to
> be carefully designed."

`verified: sugar@laptop.org message 001748, Alan Kay, 11 March 2007 — node 140 is his reply to Don's
two references on simulation software and microworld environments. The dropped text is restored from
the list copy.`

Also from node 140, Piaget by way of Kay, and the best one-line statement of why characters in this
system are not simplified adults:

> "children themselves are not homogenous in their approaches to life and learning […] it is best to
> think of children as **thinking beings in their own right rather than as 'defective adults who have
> to be fixed by education'.**"

## Two more phrases, and where the originals now live

Recovered from Don's mail archive, so the wording here is from the messages
rather than from the Drupal nodes.

**"There is nothing sacred about Etoys."** From the public sugar/devel/etoys
lists, 3 June 2007, and it is the sentence that makes the substrate-indifference
claim above credible rather than diplomatic. He follows it with the only two
things he will claim for it — that it has been tested with thousands of children
over ten years, and that its range covers children's authoring in "math and
media" — and then names its weakness himself: it is aimed at 4th–6th graders and
the UI does not fit younger ones. In the same message: *"I tried to convince the
Python community last year to take a shot at making an Etoys-like environment,
but as far as I can tell there have been no takers."*

**"A 'pre-Python' UI."** From the November 2007 letter to Don. This is the
sharpest form of the constructive half of the critique: the target is not a
scripting API, it is a notation children reach *before* they reach Python, which
the language underneath must be shaped to receive. He pairs it with the
requirement that the rules be shown "in a readable and writable form," and with
the warning that it must be **"much more than stringing black boxes together or
having to deal with fragile procedurals"** — a caution against the obvious
cheap answer, which is a node editor.

**The primary sources are archived**, with visibility marked, since one of these
is private and two are not:

| Date | Where it went | Cite? |
|---|---|---|
| 2007-03-02 | Private, to Don | **No** — use the 03-11 or 11-10 version |
| 2007-03-11 | `sugar@laptop.org`, public archives | Yes |
| 2007-06-03 | sugar + devel + etoys lists | Yes |
| 2007-11-10 | Private, then **published by Don** to the OLPC SimCity group and Slashdot | Yes, and it is the best one |

The November letter is the one to quote in public writing. It contains the
critique and the endorsement of the remedy in a single document, addressed to
the person doing the rebuild, and it carries both memorable phrases:
**"anti-ed environment"** and **"air-guitar environment."**

Lanier, forwarded the thread by Barry Threw, read it and replied: *"Cool, Alan
is right on!"* — which is worth recording because Lanier disagrees with Kay
elsewhere in the same correspondence, on whether you look into a simulated world
or stand inside it.
