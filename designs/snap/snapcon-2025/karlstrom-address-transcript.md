## The talk

**[2:38] BRIAN HARVEY:** Okay. Thank you. [Applause] Wow. Now, you're supposed to applaud at the
end, if you still feel that way. Right. Thank you, Jens. That's amazing.

**[2:50]** So here's the ACM. ACM, for those who don't know, is the Association for Computing
**Machinery** — which I joined as a high school student in 1960-something, and already then they
were embarrassed about the fact that the name made it an association for machines instead of
people, and ever since then they've been trying to find a more human-centered term that ACM would
be the acronym for.

**[3:28]** Anyway — so let me tell you about the Karlstrom Award. Here's the ACM blurb: Dan and
Brian, blah blah blah, bring the beauty and joy of computing to all students, especially those
from historically underrepresented communities. And it goes on to say how we did this through the
Beauty and Joy of Computing curriculum.

**[3:55]** So, yeah — things you don't see every day: Brian in a dinner jacket. And if you look
really carefully, you can see me holding the award upside down. Does that have a meaning? No.
Yes — yes, the meaning it has is that I'm very farsighted.

**[4:30]** Don't tell anyone, but it's really **Dan's award much more than mine**. Not only
because he's the ACM education activist — which he is, he's a big shot in the special interest
group on computer science education — but also because, if you want to know the truth, I actually
don't care that much about bringing whatever-they-said to underrepresented groups. Footnote: I'm
not saying that I'm against diversity, equity, or inclusion. I am not Donald Trump. But it's not
what excites me. **What excites me is teaching the best computer science course I can.** And I
kind of have faith that if I can do that, then even girls and people of color will find it
interesting — because it IS interesting.

**[5:44]** So what is the best computer science course that I know how to teach? For BJC this
means **functional programming** — recursion, higher order functions. These are the secret sauce
of computer science.

**[6:18]** Before BJC, Matt Wright and I wrote the course that it replaced at Berkeley, called
*Simply Scheme*. We designed it as a prequel to what is the actual best computer science course
ever invented: *Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs*, by Hal Abelson and Gerald Jay
Sussman with Julie Sussman. They wrote the book 60, 70 years ago, something like that — and it's
still the best computer science course ever invented, in my humble opinion.

**[6:59]** This goal — teaching functional programming — had an awful lot to do with how BYOB 3
and then Snap! looks. So we have the gray rings around procedures, and all of that. **That's
lambda.** And that was really my contribution to Snap! more than anything else: anonymous
procedures in Snap!. So it's Dan's award much more than mine — but I'm up here rather than Dan
because this is a Snap! conference, not a BJC conference.

### On AI in education

**[7:48]** Okay. Jens wanted me to predict the future for you — say something radical about
what's going to happen in computer science education. I think AI may be profoundly world-changing
for adults who need jobs to stay alive. That's a thing that could happen. But I'm not sure how
much it's going to change the lives of children — because schooling is remarkably tough,
persistent.

**[8:30]** Mostly, people who talk about AI in education see it as a threat: kids will use it to
cheat; or maybe AI will replace teachers so we won't have jobs anymore; or — sort of the best of
these views — we don't know yet what skills kids are really going to need in an AI-dominated
world, supposing that we have one.

**[9:00]** I'm pretty confident that AI won't replace teachers for rich kids — and **computers
already replace teachers for poor kids**, long before AI. The poor kids sit in front of computers
that give them arithmetic problems. So it's not as if we're opening up a can of worms that's
brand new.

**[9:33]** Why do we still make kids learn arithmetic? People I respect, such as Paul Goldenberg,
argue that mathematical understanding has to start with the concrete before the abstract becomes
understandable — that if you just give 8-year-olds an algebra course, they won't get it; they
have to be comfortable doing arithmetic first. That's an argument — I mean, maybe that's right.
Paul's career, at least in recent years, has largely been about making arithmetic not be the huge
turnoff that it is for most kids. Still, to me this feels a bit like those ancient Greeks who
argued that kids had to learn to memorize poetry even though they had smartphones.

**[10:46]** Time was, when I got started in this business, everyone said that kids had to learn
to use a word processor — instead of programming — lest they be unemployable. Anybody my age
remember those days? And there were big arguments about WHICH word processor we should teach the
kids, because if you learn one, you can't use another one. What happened wasn't exactly that they
were proven wrong, but rather that kids now grow up in a world in which word processing is no big
deal. They just learn to do it. If you can play those complicated video games that kids play that
I can't, you can use a word processor.

**[11:42]** Similarly, I think that if it turns out kids have to know something about how to use
AI software, then a generation from now they will know. It won't be a big deal. This is one of
these transitional issues rather than a big thing. So no, I don't think AI is going to change
much in education. I could be wrong.

### On curriculum

**[12:05]** So I want to talk about curriculum, which is sort of the big bugaboo of my life. Dan
and I got the Karlstrom award for the Beauty and Joy of Computing, which isn't just any
curriculum — it's one that's attached to a College Board Advanced Placement exam, AP Computer
Science Principles. Which means that every five years, when they change the rules for what has to
be in AP CS Principles, we have to give them a spreadsheet in which we show them exactly where in
BJC each of the 200-odd bullet points is covered. Pain in the butt.

**[13:00]** But in fact, writing curriculum is what I've mostly done with my life. It's certainly
the thing I'm known for: *Computer Science Logo Style*, *Simply Scheme*, BJC. Even the so-called
Snap! Reference Manual is full of tutorial curriculum about some of the more abstract features —
starting with first class lists (what does it mean for lists to be first class?), and then first
class functions, object-oriented programming and inheritance, first class continuations — I'm
kind of pleased that we actually have that — and metaprogramming, recently.

**[13:51]** Many of the teachers who use BJC press us to add **more assessment**. That's what
they want in a curriculum. They want problems they can give kids and grade them on, so that they
can assign a grade at the end of the course — and, for that matter, so that they can make their
principals happy week by week. They have to keep on assessing kids. And because of that, they get
very upset if kids find solutions to the exercises online — which was a big problem when we
invented smart script pics in Snap!, because the script pics in the curriculum were largely made
from the secret solutions projects rather than from the stripped-down starter projects. So every
one of those pictures has the complete solution in it. Horror.

**[15:20]** So the fact of curriculum pushes people toward making it like SCHOOL, and not like
progressive education. For example, here's a thing called a self-check question, from BJC. This
is actually one of the better ones, because you're not just supposed to memorize the answer —
you're supposed to figure it out as you read the code. But the context of this question is a lab
about generating English sentences. (We used to say "natural language sentences" so as not to be
English-centric, but I've had a lot of lectures from Jens about how this sentence generation
exercise doesn't work in German. So I guess I'm back to saying English.)

**[16:31]** But sentence generation isn't what this question is about. This question is about how
to write a program using random numbers to pick different alternatives with different
probabilities. Which is fine — that's a technique — but it's not the point of the lab, and having
questions like this in the lab **distracts from the point of the lab**. In the beginning we had
very, very rare self-check questions: every so often something would come up where we thought
kids were being asked to learn something complicated, and we wanted an opportunity for them to
stop and see whether they'd learned it or not. But nowadays every single page in the curriculum
is full of self-check questions. And furthermore, the name "self-check question" has become a
lie — because it's not for checking yourself, it's for your teacher to give you a grade. So **it
should really be called "jump through hoops questions."** It always bothers me when curricula lie
to the students, and this is an example of that.

### CCUS — the real life's work

**[18:09]** So I'm not 100% pleased that I'm being recognized, in retrospect, for having written
a lot of curriculum. That's not what I think is the best thing I ever did. **What they should
have given me an award for is the Computer Center Users Society.**

**[18:39]** So — down in the front, kneeling down, that's me on the right and Paul Goldenberg on
the left (your left). But more importantly, we have **my friend Deepy, and my friend Dan, and my
friend Dominic, and my friend Will, and my friend Jonathan, and my friend Prabhu, and my friend
Robert, and my friend Michael, and my friend Kenny, and my friend Jay** — who I think Bernat
looks exactly like, but never mind. And I'm not going to go through the back row, but I could.
And I didn't have to look them up. Because, you know, 20 years later, 30 years later, however
long it's been — **these are my friends.**

**[19:37]** So: the Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School in Sudbury, Massachusetts. If you go in
the middle of the night, not during rush hour, it's about a half-hour drive from MIT. When I
decided to stop reading about teaching and actually do it, it took a while for me to find a
school where I thought I'd be able to be ME, and not be a stereotype teacher. But eventually,
through mutual friends — namely Paul and Cindy — I did find this school, and amazingly, they
wanted a computer teacher rather than a math teacher, which I thought I would have to be. I
thought I was giving up computers when I entered teaching. So I got there in January 1979 to set
up a computer department.

**[20:35]** They had a computer back then, sort of run by the math department. But the school —
including the math teachers who ran this facility — had the idea that calling it part of the math
department turned off kids who hated math, which is, to a first approximation, kids. They thought
that if we had a separate computer department, kids would find it appealing. And that worked,
pretty much — we did get a lot of kids who would not have been so interested in something
presented to them as mathematics.

**[21:25]** But the important thing that happened is that **I talked the school into letting me
not give grades.** I would never get away with that today. But you have to remember, in 1979
computers hadn't taken over the world yet, and so the school thought of computing as a fun
elective, like drama or art, rather than as a serious core subject. That's why they let me get
away with it.

**[21:56]** So everything was great — except for two problems. The first problem was **litter**
in the computer center. About the same time as this was all happening, businesses started
advertising the paperless office. They were wrong. Computers have not done away with paper; they
generate paper much faster than human beings ever could. So, litter — to the point where the
janitors refused to walk into the computer center. Which was a crisis. And the second problem
was: I would get to school at 7 in the morning — classes started at 8 — and when I got there,
there'd be **a dozen kids lined up in front of the computer center** waiting for me to let them
in. And when school was over in the afternoon, they wouldn't let me go home, because they wanted
to keep hacking on the computer.

**[23:00]** So my colleague **Larry Davidson had the brilliant idea to use these two problems to
solve each other.** We established the Computer Center Users Society, which would be in charge of
running the room — general meetings to make policy, but specifically including responsibility for
keeping the room clean. (Parenthesis: I was hoping they would do it by teaching themselves to
throw their garbage in the garbage. But no — they set up a rotating schedule, with a different
kid each day in charge of cleaning up at the end of the day.)

**[23:46]** Why would anybody be part of the society? **Because members were given keys to the
computer center.** Another thing I'd never get away with today. Giving kids keys to any room
would be problematic enough — but a room full of expensive computer equipment, and I want to give
kids keys? Yes.

**[24:08]** One more thing I'd never get away with: taking kids out to dinner. Even though kids
had keys to the room and I was therefore allowed to go home, I still didn't — because this was
where my friends were, in the computer center. And every so often I would say, "Hey, let's go out
for Chinese food." And there were enough seniors — kids old enough to have driver's licenses —
that we had enough cars that everybody could pile in, and we would drive to Cambridge, where the
good Chinese restaurants were.

**[25:07]** Another thing I'd never get away with: taking kids home for the weekend. No, they did
not sleep in my bed — I am not Michael Jackson. They slept in the fold-out couch in my living
room. But, you know, I taught them to eat eggs Benedict and stuff like that. It was great.

**[25:33]** And remember, the school was in Massachusetts. In the summer, Massachusetts not only
has muggy humidity but also flying insects. So I would go to California for the summer — and in
two different summers, **I took a few kids out to California with me.** And boy, this is
definitely something I'd never get away with today. Today it's just assumed that if you're an
unmarried male teacher and you want to see kids socially, it's to abuse them.

**[26:18]** Indeed — after I got this award, the ACM magazine has a feature called People of ACM,
and they interviewed me. They do the interview by email; they sent out a bunch of questions and I
sent back answers. And in what I sent them, I told them the story of the Lincoln-Sudbury Computer
Center Users Society, just as I've told it to you here. **But they edited out the stuff on this
page.** They didn't want to talk about me taking kids out to dinner, let alone out to California.
**So, I hate the 21st century. Thank you.** [Applause]
