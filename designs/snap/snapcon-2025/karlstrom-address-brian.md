## Jens's introduction

Jens introduced Brian as collaborator and "the world's best programming teacher," recalling Brian
at MIT Sloan saying **"computing is your birthright as a human being"** — a line that stuck with
everyone in the room. He asked for radical, non-conformist, maybe mundane visions about
education, computing, and AI.

## The hook

Brian uses the Karlstrom podium not to polish the award, but to argue that **curriculum is not
his proudest life's work** — the **Lincoln-Sudbury Computer Center Users Society (CCUS)** is.
Grades, assessment spreadsheets, and institutional fear have made the personal teacher-student
friendship he lived in 1979 nearly impossible today.

## Award honesty

- "It's really **Dan's award** much more than mine" — Dan Garcia is the ACM education activist;
  Brian is at Snap!Con, not a BJC conference.
- The award blurb's DEI framing is not what excites him (he's not against it — "I am not Donald
  Trump"). **Teaching the best CS course he can**, and trusting that interesting material reaches
  everyone, is the excitement.
- Holding the award upside down in the photo: "Farsighted — no deeper symbolism."

## ACM: machines, not people

Brian opens by noting ACM is the Association for Computing **Machinery** — he joined as a high
school student in the 1960s, and even then ACM was embarrassed the name made it an association
for machines instead of people. Parallel: **Ben Shneiderman** answered the same accident the
other way, founding the **Human**-Computer Interaction Laboratory (human first, not CHIL) — see
HCIL human-first naming. Brian's
CCUS story and "every kid needs a *me* teacher" are the pedagogy version of the same correction:
put the human first, not the machine, not the grade, not the assessment spreadsheet.

## The best CS course

*Simply Scheme* (with Matt Wright) as prequel to SICP — "still the best computer science course
ever invented." The secret sauce: functional programming — recursion, higher-order functions.
Brian's main technical contribution to Snap! (BYOB 3 → Snap!): **anonymous procedures** — the
gray rings. **That's lambda.**

## AI in education

AI may be profoundly world-changing for adults who need jobs; it probably won't change children's
schooling much. Like word processors: everyone once said kids must learn WordPerfect or be
unemployable; kids now grow up where word processing is no big deal. AI won't replace teachers
for rich kids — and **computers already replace teachers for poor kids** (arithmetic drill
software), long before AI. A transitional issue, not a deep change. "I could be wrong."

## Curriculum critique

- Writing curriculum is what Brian is known for — *CS Logo Style*, *Simply Scheme*, BJC, the
  Snap! Reference Manual's tutorial chapters — but it's "the big bugaboo of my life."
- BJC is tied to College Board AP CS Principles: every five years, a spreadsheet mapping 200+
  bullet points to curriculum pages. "Pain in the butt."
- Teachers want more assessment — problems to grade, principals happy week by week. Self-check
  questions proliferated until the name became a lie: **"It should really be called jump through
  hoops questions."** Curricula shouldn't lie to students.
- Smart script pics horror: the curriculum's script pics were built from secret solution
  projects — every thumbnail embeds the complete answer.

## CCUS — the real life's work

Lincoln-Sudbury Regional High School, January 1979. The school treated computing as a fun
elective like drama or art, so Brian negotiated **no grades** — that made friendship possible;
with grades you're a hurdle, not a friend. Two problems (litter so bad the janitors refused to
enter; kids lined up at 7am and wouldn't go home) solved each other via **Larry Davidson's**
idea: the Computer Center Users Society runs the room — general meetings, rotating cleanup,
**keys to the room** for members. Chinese food runs to Cambridge, kids to California for
summers, eggs Benedict, the fold-out couch ("I am not Michael Jackson"). ACM's People of ACM
interview edited the dinner-and-California material out: **"So, I hate the 21st century. Thank
you."** In the CCUS photo Brian named every student **"my friend [Name]"** — deliberately,
against institutions that conspire to make teachers the enemies of kids.

## Q&A highlights

- **The Dutch math teacher** — had Brian-style teachers in the 70s (slept at their houses,
  holidays together); the 21st century forbade him the same; "Thank you for celebrating the
  20th century."
- **Dan Garcia** — 35 years of mentorship; after-school clubs and Berkeley DeCals persist the
  Lincoln-Sudbury spirit. Brian: the daytime hoop-jumping computers still exist alongside.
- **Relationship over content** — "When you're giving somebody grades, you can't be their
  friend… you're a hurdle they have to overcome." One Black teacher in twelve years vastly
  improves a Black kid's outcomes — through **connection**, not "Black stuff." The moderator's
  takeaway: teachers have the purpose of building a relationship — holistic, ways to lead lives
  together — and offered Brian **Martin Buber** as the philosopher of exactly this (the
  questioner's pointer, not Brian's own citation).
- **The seventh-grade algebra teacher** — "If you listen to the long version, I talk about my
  seventh grade algebra teacher, who was the best teacher I had, and who didn't actually teach
  me anything. There's a story about that." Then: **"Not every teacher has to be me, but every
  kid has to have a ME teacher."**
- **Hargreaves and unconditional positive regard** — an audience member: Brian embodies
  Hargreaves's "unconditional positive regard for your students, irrespective of any kind of
  relationship issues"; the assessment problem "never goes away — you just have to tackle it,
  and not assume there's some magic solution." Brian, honestly: "There were kids I didn't like…
  I was not a good teacher for those kids. So, you know — not perfect." Pushback: you're not
  ALLOWED to not like a kid when you're a teacher — you have to be fair in your grading.
- **Educators shape CS itself** — same audience member: Brian is "the embodiment of somebody who
  has shaped computer science itself through your educative purpose" — educators can help shape
  computer science, "not simply accept it from some professionals that think they know what
  they're doing." Lambda in Snap! is the proof: pedagogy drove the language.
