# Dog-walking Steve Jobs from "That sucks!" to "Wow, that's great!"

`verified: EDUCOM, Washington Hilton, Washington DC, October 1988. Primary
sources: Don's own email written hours after the demo (to lectroids@ucbvax,
1988-10-26 06:31 GMT+1), his retelling six months later (to the GoodNeWS author,
1989-04-22), Ben Shneiderman's independent account to his lab list
(1988-11-01), and Ben's logistics mail setting up the booth slot (1988-10-22).
Superseded earlier drafts of this file that mis-dated Don's email and merged two
separate demos.`

The case study in this directory that is *not* about a comment box. Same
problem: a person who has already decided your work is worthless, saying so, out
loud, repeatedly. Different answer than arguing.

## What happened

**The night before the floor opened**, Don carried a quarter-inch tape to the
Washington Hilton and loaded his portable PostScript demo onto a 24 MB Sun
3/60C at the Sun booth. The machine he was working on faced the NeXT booth
diagonally. The NeXT booth was still curtained, with synthesized music coming
from inside.

Ben Shneiderman had arranged the slot, and Sun's terms are worth recording
because they set the register: the representative "must be dedicated to the SUN
booth during that time and must be dressed in business attire." Ben's comment to
his lab, in full: *"I'll let you interpret the final phrase."*

The scheduled Hyperties slot was Tuesday 8–10pm, following an Open Look/NeWS
demo at 6–8pm that Ben flagged as "worth seeing."

**On the show floor**, in Don's words, *"Ben Shneiderman and I pinned down Steve
Jobs and led him over to the Sun booth."* Ben's version: *"I invited him to see
our Hyperties SUN version that we were showing at the SUN booth."* Roughly half
an hour, both accounts agree. NeWS, HyperTIES, UniPress Emacs on NeWS, pie
menus, and, in Don's phrase, *"a bunch of other PostScript based goodies."* Don
gave him a NeRD button.

Don on the half hour: *"I did my share of talking and foaming at the mouth, and
he did his."* Afterwards: *"I had a wonderful oxygen buzz from raving and waving
my arms around."*

### Two demos, not one

**The Bill Joy's Head demo was a separate demo, and the order of the two is not
remembered.** Don was giving revolving demos to a stream of people across
several days of the show — exhausting, and by his own account fun, energetic and
motivated the whole time.

What the Jobs demo *did* include was the argument about arbitrary window shapes,
which Don lost: *"I just wasn't able to convince him how useful it was to be
able to have windows in the shape of the Hubble Space Telescope in Orbit, or
Bill Joy's Head."* The demo where Joy himself was revealed after the Emacs
demos, freshly shaved, was a different session. An earlier version of this file
welded the two together, which is how a good anecdote goes wrong: two true
things at one event become one thing that did not happen.

## The sequence, from the contemporaneous record

| Source | Written | As recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Don → lectroids | hours after, 1988-10-26 | "That sucks! That sucks! That sucks! Wow, that's great!" |
| Don → GoodNeWS | six months later, 1989-04-22 | "That sucks! That sucks! Wow, that's neat! That sucks!" |
| Ben Shneiderman → his lab | six days after, 1988-11-01 | "going from positive comments such as 'Great!' to 'That sucks'" |

**The account written the same night is the record.** Three dismissals, then
"Wow, that's great!" Don's own note on the discrepancy, after re-reading it:
*"aha that does say great so maybe I misremembered it. Let's go with what that
contemporaneous email says."*

**The six-month version is not thrown away, because it preserves something the
first one flattens.** It ends on another "that sucks," and Don's recollection is
that a "neat" followed even that. So the arc was not a conversion — it was a man
reacting item by item, approving some and dismissing others, and finishing
approving. Ben's independent account brackets it from the other direction,
recording movement in *both* directions across the half hour.

Reconciled: **the two accounts do not conflict about the ending, only about how
many times the needle swung before it got there.** That is a better story than
the monotonic one, and the difference between two tellings by the same person six
months apart is exactly the read wear that Article VI says to keep rather than
sand off.

Ben's note adds the detail that matters most, verbatim:

> "he had a terrific sensitivity to the user interface issues and could
> articulate his reasons wonderfully"

The dismissals were not reflexive. **He was a good critic in a bad mood.** Don
independently: *"He made a lot of very good points, and boy did he argue them
well! The man has RELIGION!"*

Don's account of why it worked: *"I didn't let him discourage me and I kept on
target. And had a lot of things to show that all fit together as a whole."*

## The flexibility line

From the April 1989 email, verbatim, typo and all:

> "I don't need flexability -- I got my window system right the first time!"

**Note what the contemporaneous wording actually says: *window system*, not
*user interface*.** Narrower and sharper, and a direct rebuttal to the
malleable-computing position rather than a general boast.

Don's gloss, decades later: *"Be that as it may … Yet here we are. Still not
perfect. Still measuring."*

## Why he was dismissing, which is not why he said he was

The timing is the context, and it is sharper than the version usually told.
**Jobs had unveiled the NeXT Computer thirteen days earlier**, at Davies
Symphony Hall on 1988-10-12, and at EDUCOM he was showing it. Don saw it there
and wanted one.

**But it had not shipped, and would not for months.** The *Los Angeles Times*,
reporting the unveiling the next morning, noted the machine "won't be generally
available until spring." The first units — about 200, to roughly ten
universities — were delivered at the end of December 1988, running developer
software numbered 0.8 precisely to signal it was not ready. NeXTSTEP 1.0 and
broad availability came in 1989.

`verified: LA Times 1988-10-13; Chicago Tribune, January 1989, on the first
university deliveries. This corrects Don's recollection that Jobs "had just
shipped" — a natural compression thirty-odd years later, and the correction
makes the story stronger rather than weaker.`

**So the "NeVR STEP" mockery was still live.** He had not ended the delay; he
had announced the end of it, standing next to a machine nobody could buy, and
the shirts were in the room. Which explains the defensiveness better than "he
had just won" does — and it puts the flexibility line in company with his own
answer when asked whether the delay bothered him:

> "Late? This computer is five years ahead of its time!"

`verified: widely reproduced; see the NeXT article on Wikipedia for the sourcing
chain — treat as attributed-but-secondary`

Same move twice in the same fortnight: take the criticism and re-describe it as
the achievement. It is genuinely effective and it is not an argument.

**The dismissal had a cause that was not about the work**, and that is the
general case rather than the exception. It predicts behavior far better than the
content of what was said. Nobody's opinion of a demo produces "that sucks" three
times before the demo is over.

This is the same structure as a drive-by cliché. "Just a next token predictor"
is rarely a conclusion someone reached; it is a position they arrived holding,
for reasons that have nothing to do with your comment.

### And Don was not the partisan the story wants him to be

The thing that makes this a case study rather than a war story: **he loved the
machine.** From the same email as the "that sucks" quotes, written the same
night:

> "I saw the NeXT machine! I'm not leaving school before I get one. … But boy
> what a sight -- 1' x 1' x 1' black silicon sex."

He went back the next day to try to get his hands on one. So the person arguing
hardest for NeWS was simultaneously trying to acquire the competing product out
of straightforward enthusiasm.

**That is the whole `STEELMAN` discipline, thirty-odd years early and done
without a protocol.** Granting the other side's achievement in full, in
writing, on the same page as the disagreement, costs nothing and is the reason
the disagreement reads as technical rather than tribal.

## What transfers to the skill

**Don't argue with the dismissal. Keep showing.** Three "that sucks" answered
with three rebuttals is a fight, and the audience remembers a fight. Three
answered with the next thing, and the next thing, is a demo — and it worked.

**Coherence is the argument.** Not one killer feature, but many pieces that fit
together as a whole. The whole is the thing a dismissal cannot get purchase on,
because each piece answers a different objection and none of them is the
load-bearing one. Ten quotes in a comment is a wall; ten working parts in a
system is a case. The corpus is for building the second kind.

**The win condition is "that's great", not "I was wrong".** Jobs never conceded
anything. He did not retract "that sucks", did not agree NeWS was better, and
went home to build NeXT. He just stopped dismissing. That is the whole victory
available and it is worth having.

This is exactly the goal SKILL.md already states — **they should stop, not
lose** — with a receipt from decades before the skill existed. Anyone writing a
reply that aims at making the other person lose should read this first and
notice that the successful version did not.

**Persistence without escalation.** "Kept on target" is doing the work in that
sentence. He did not get louder, did not get personal, and did not stop.

**The skeptic may be a good critic in a bad mood.** Ben's independent note is
the correction to the comfortable reading of this story. Assuming the dismissal
is stupid is the one move guaranteed to lose, and the corpus is built to make
the opposite assumption cheap: harvest the steelman first, and you find out
whether you are dealing with a bad argument or a good one badly delivered.

**Endurance is a demo too.** Ben's account notes Jobs came out to the Maryland
lab the following day for more demos, and that he "had little patience with the
academic side of things, but was very ready to engage over interface issues."
The engagement continued past the half hour on the floor, which is the actual
measure of whether a hostile session went well — not what was said at the time.

## What it was worth, long after

The lasting effect was not on Jobs. Don's own summary: the rejections and the
eventual concessions **gave him the confidence to stick with his ideas** — and
many years later, he is still on it.

That is the real return on refusing to be talked out of something in public. Not
that the skeptic converts, but that you learn your work survives contact with a
hostile expert. The corpus is the same bet at a smaller scale.

One postscript on what the work was worth to the people who were there. Ben, to
Sun, three weeks later, asking them to fund it:

> "The team includes Don Hopkins whose efforts, especially in creating pie
> menus, are widely known."

## Related

Alan Kay's general form of "yet here we are," now cited and verbatim in
[`../corpus/CANON.md`](../corpus/CANON.md), from an email to Don that Don
published on HN in 2014:

> "Things seem to hang on in computing just because they work a little bit."

Which is the answer to "I got my window system right the first time," and it
does not require anyone to have been wrong — only to notice that surviving and
being right are different achievements.
