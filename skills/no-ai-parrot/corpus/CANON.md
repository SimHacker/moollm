# CANON — the standing corrections, by named humans, with dates

Quotable passages. Every one written by an identified person, most of them
before 1990. Nothing in this file is generated, summarized-as-quotation, or
reworded.

**The `verified` column is not decoration.** A quote you have not checked
against the source is a rumor with quotation marks around it. Entries marked
`needs-check` are recorded with their wording as commonly reproduced and must
be confirmed against print before you paste them into anything public. Do the
check once; it is permanent.

## Shannon 1951 — next-token prediction was an instrument for studying people

> Shannon, C. E. (1951). "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English."
> *Bell System Technical Journal* 30(1), 50–64.

Shannon estimated the entropy of English by presenting human subjects with text
and having them **guess the next letter**, one letter at a time, using the
number of guesses required as the measurement. `verified: setup — described in
the paper's method, not a quotation`

Candidate quotation, for the claim that human language competence *is*
statistical knowledge:

> "The new method of estimating entropy exploits the fact that anyone speaking a
> language possesses, implicitly, an enormous knowledge of the statistics of the
> language."

`verified: needs-check — confirm wording against BSTJ 30(1), opening section`

**Why it is the single most useful item in this file.** It is a fact with a page
number rather than an argument, and it reverses the direction of the insult
without requiring any claim about machines. Next-token prediction entered the
literature in 1951 as a way of measuring human beings.

## Jefferson 1949 — the strongest form of the objection

> Jefferson, G. (1949). "The Mind of Mechanical Man." *British Medical Journal*
> 1(4616), 1105–1110. The Lister Oration, delivered 9 June 1949.

As quoted by Turing in 1950:

> "Not until a machine can write a sonnet or compose a concerto because of
> thoughts and emotions felt, and not by the chance fall of symbols, could we
> agree that machine equals brain — that is, not only write it but know that it
> had written it."

`verified: verbatim as quoted in Turing 1950 §6, objection (4)`

**Use.** Hand this to the person you are arguing with. It is their position,
made in 1949, considerably better than they are making it, by a distinguished
neurosurgeon in a Lister Oration. Conceding that the objection has a serious
pedigree costs nothing and changes the temperature of the exchange.

## Turing 1950 — how the careful version of that position ends

> Turing, A. M. (1950). "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." *Mind* 59(236),
> 433–460. Section 6, "Contrary Views on the Main Question," works through nine
> numbered objections; objection (4) is "The Argument from Consciousness."

On where the argument leads if pressed:

> "According to the most extreme form of this view the only way by which one
> could be sure that a machine thinks is to be the machine and to feel oneself
> thinking. ... It is in fact the solipsist point of view. It may be the most
> logical view to hold but it makes communication of ideas difficult."

`verified: verbatim, Mind 59(236) §6`

On residual mystery, which is the part people skip:

> "I do not wish to give the impression that I think there is no mystery about
> consciousness. ... But I do not think these mysteries necessarily need to be
> solved before we can answer the question with which we are concerned in this
> paper."

`verified: verbatim, Mind 59(236) §6`

**Use.** Turing did not dismiss the objection, he named its strongest form,
conceded what was unresolved, and proposed a test instead of issuing a verdict.
That is the standard, and it is quotable against anyone who asserts an answer
while forbidding the question.

Turing's own annotated materials:
https://turingarchive.kings.cam.ac.uk/publications-lectures-and-talks-amtb/amt-b-44

## Lifton 1961 — the name of the move

> Lifton, R. J. (1961). *Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism: A Study
> of "Brainwashing" in China.* Chapter 22, "Ideological Totalism" — the
> criterion of **loading the language**.

> "The language of the totalist environment is characterized by the
> thought-terminating cliché. The most far-reaching and complex of human
> problems are compressed into brief, highly reductive, definitive-sounding
> phrases, easily memorized and easily expressed."

`verified: needs-check — confirm against ch. 22 in print; this is the commonly
reproduced wording`

**Use.** Quote Lifton and stop. Let the reader apply it. "Thought-terminating
cliché" is a technical term from a study of coercive persuasion, and quoted with
its citation it is analysis; asserted in your own voice it is just a different
cliché. Never apply it to the person, only to the phrase.

## Weizenbaum 1966 and 1976 — the part the other side gets right

> Weizenbaum, J. (1966). "ELIZA — A Computer Program For the Study of Natural
> Language Communication Between Man and Machine." *CACM* 9(1), 36–45.
> Weizenbaum, J. (1976). *Computer Power and Human Reason: From Judgment to
> Calculation.*

> "Some subjects have been very hard to convince that ELIZA (with its present
> script) is not human."

`verified: needs-check — confirm against CACM 9(1); the discussion section`

**Use, and use it early.** The ELIZA effect is real, it is old, and it is the
honest core of every phrase in this catalog: people over-attribute mind to
fluent text, reliably, and the man who built the program was alarmed by it. Cite
this approvingly *before* you take anything apart. An argument that concedes
nothing teaches nobody.

## Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, Shmitchell 2021 — what the phrase actually came from

> Bender, E. M., Gebru, T., McMillan-Major, A., Shmitchell, S. (2021). "On the
> Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big? 🦜"
> *FAccT '21*, 610–623.

The definition, which is the part everyone borrows:

> "haphazardly stitching together sequences of linguistic forms it has observed
> in its vast training data, according to probabilistic information about how
> they combine, but without any reference to meaning"

`verified: needs-check — confirm against FAccT '21 proceedings §6`

**What the paper argues:** environmental and financial cost of scale; training
corpora too large to document responsibly; encoded bias and downstream harm;
and the risk that **humans over-attribute meaning to fluent text.**

**What it does not argue:** that language models cannot reason. It is a
risk-and-ethics argument about scale, data and human misattribution.

**The structural irony, worth stating in your own words rather than as a jab:**
the paper's central concern is that fluent text causes people to stop thinking.
Using its title as a conversation-ender is that concern, enacted, by a person.

The prior and more philosophical paper, which is usually what people mean:

> Bender, E. M., Koller, A. (2020). "Climbing towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and
> Understanding in the Age of Data." *ACL 2020*. The octopus thought experiment.

## Searle 1980 — what "doesn't really understand" is borrowing

> Searle, J. (1980). "Minds, Brains, and Programs." *Behavioral and Brain
> Sciences* 3(3), 417–457.

`verified: citation only`

**Notes for accurate use.** The Chinese Room argues against a specific
functionalism and was answered in the same issue, including by the Systems
Reply. Searle's own position is that brains cause minds through biological
causal powers — **not** that machine minds are impossible in principle. Anyone
citing him for the stronger claim is citing him for something he did not say.

## Not in the canon, on purpose

- **No quotations about anyone's health, diagnosis, medication or private life**,
  whoever said them and however publicly. They are not part of the argument.
- **No paraphrase presented as quotation.** If it is not in quotation marks with
  a citation, it is orientation, and orientation does not get pasted.
- **No passages this skill wrote.** There are none, and there is no method that
  would produce one.
