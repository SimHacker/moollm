# CANON — the standing corrections, by named humans, with dates

Quotable passages. Every one written by an identified person, most of them
before 1990. Nothing in this file is generated, summarized-as-quotation, or
reworded.

**The `verified` column is not decoration.** A quote you have not checked
against the source is a rumor with quotation marks around it. Entries marked
`needs-check` are recorded with their wording as commonly reproduced and must
be confirmed against print before you paste them into anything public. Do the
check once; it is permanent.

## Shannon 1951 — next-token prediction was an instrument for studying people

> Shannon, C. E. (1951). "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English."
> *Bell System Technical Journal* 30(1), 50–64.

Shannon estimated the entropy of English by presenting human subjects with text
and having them **guess the next letter**, one letter at a time, using the
number of guesses required as the measurement. `verified: setup — described in
the paper's method, not a quotation`

Candidate quotation, for the claim that human language competence *is*
statistical knowledge:

> "The new method of estimating entropy exploits the fact that anyone speaking a
> language possesses, implicitly, an enormous knowledge of the statistics of the
> language."

`verified: needs-check — confirm wording against BSTJ 30(1), opening section`

**Why it is the single most useful item in this file.** It is a fact with a page
number rather than an argument, and it reverses the direction of the insult
without requiring any claim about machines. Next-token prediction entered the
literature in 1951 as a way of measuring human beings.

## Jefferson 1949 — the strongest form of the objection

> Jefferson, G. (1949). "The Mind of Mechanical Man." *British Medical Journal*
> 1(4616), 1105–1110. The Lister Oration, delivered 9 June 1949.

As quoted by Turing in 1950:

> "Not until a machine can write a sonnet or compose a concerto because of
> thoughts and emotions felt, and not by the chance fall of symbols, could we
> agree that machine equals brain — that is, not only write it but know that it
> had written it."

`verified: verbatim as quoted in Turing 1950 §6, objection (4)`

**Use.** Hand this to the person you are arguing with. It is their position,
made in 1949, considerably better than they are making it, by a distinguished
neurosurgeon in a Lister Oration. Conceding that the objection has a serious
pedigree costs nothing and changes the temperature of the exchange.

## Turing 1950 — how the careful version of that position ends

> Turing, A. M. (1950). "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." *Mind* 59(236),
> 433–460. Section 6, "Contrary Views on the Main Question," works through nine
> numbered objections; objection (4) is "The Argument from Consciousness."

On where the argument leads if pressed:

> "According to the most extreme form of this view the only way by which one
> could be sure that a machine thinks is to be the machine and to feel oneself
> thinking. ... It is in fact the solipsist point of view. It may be the most
> logical view to hold but it makes communication of ideas difficult."

`verified: verbatim, Mind 59(236) §6`

On residual mystery, which is the part people skip:

> "I do not wish to give the impression that I think there is no mystery about
> consciousness. ... But I do not think these mysteries necessarily need to be
> solved before we can answer the question with which we are concerned in this
> paper."

`verified: verbatim, Mind 59(236) §6`

**Use.** Turing did not dismiss the objection, he named its strongest form,
conceded what was unresolved, and proposed a test instead of issuing a verdict.
That is the standard, and it is quotable against anyone who asserts an answer
while forbidding the question.

Turing's own annotated materials:
https://turingarchive.kings.cam.ac.uk/publications-lectures-and-talks-amtb/amt-b-44

## Lifton 1961 — the name of the move

> Lifton, R. J. (1961). *Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism: A Study
> of "Brainwashing" in China.* Chapter 22, "Ideological Totalism" — the
> criterion of **loading the language**.

> "The language of the totalist environment is characterized by the
> thought-terminating cliché. The most far-reaching and complex of human
> problems are compressed into brief, highly reductive, definitive-sounding
> phrases, easily memorized and easily expressed."

`verified: needs-check — confirm against ch. 22 in print; this is the commonly
reproduced wording`

**Use.** Quote Lifton and stop. Let the reader apply it. "Thought-terminating
cliché" is a technical term from a study of coercive persuasion, and quoted with
its citation it is analysis; asserted in your own voice it is just a different
cliché. Never apply it to the person, only to the phrase.

### ANTI-PATTERN: quoting someone's line, then Lifton, with no commentary

This looks like the most economical possible reply and it is the worst available
use of this entry. **Do not do it.** Three reasons, and the first is fatal:

1. **The juxtaposition *is* the commentary, and it lands on the person.** Two
   quotes side by side with nothing between them says *you are exhibiting
   ideological totalism* — a far heavier charge than *that phrase is doing no
   work* — while leaving no sentence anyone can hold you to. Saying it by
   arrangement rather than in words does not make it neutral. It makes it
   deniable, which is worse, and it violates the last line of the Use note
   above by the only means available.
2. **It answers a label with a label.** The whole case against the cliché is
   that no claim follows it. A reply with no claim in it fails its own test,
   and the mirror-image objection is correct: it is a thought-terminating
   response to a thought-terminating cliché, with a better footnote.
3. **The proportion is wrong and readers will see it.** Lifton is studying
   coercive persuasion in Maoist thought reform. Aiming it at a snarky
   one-liner is a category escalation, and the register mismatch is what the
   audience notices — not the citation's aptness. It also aims at making the
   person lose, which is the goal `SKILL.md` explicitly rejects: **they should
   stop, not lose.**

**What brevity should be spent on instead.** One clause conceding the objective,
then *one claim*, then at most one citation. The short forms that work:

- The Shannon reversal, which is a fact with a page number and accuses nobody.
- The path question from [`ARGUMENTS.md`](ARGUMENTS.md) — *shaped by what
  path?* — which is one sentence and invites inspection rather than deference.
- Nothing at all, and a link to something that works. That is the EDUCOM
  lesson in [`../examples/educom-jobs.md`](../examples/educom-jobs.md): don't
  argue with the dismissal, keep showing.

## Weizenbaum 1966 and 1976 — the part the other side gets right

> Weizenbaum, J. (1966). "ELIZA — A Computer Program For the Study of Natural
> Language Communication Between Man and Machine." *CACM* 9(1), 36–45.
> Weizenbaum, J. (1976). *Computer Power and Human Reason: From Judgment to
> Calculation.*

> "Some subjects have been very hard to convince that ELIZA (with its present
> script) is not human."

`verified: needs-check — confirm against CACM 9(1); the discussion section`

**Use, and use it early.** The ELIZA effect is real, it is old, and it is the
honest core of every phrase in this catalog: people over-attribute mind to
fluent text, reliably, and the man who built the program was alarmed by it. Cite
this approvingly *before* you take anything apart. An argument that concedes
nothing teaches nobody.

## MacKay's Dasher — the same prediction, pointed the other way

> Dasher. David J. C. MacKay and the Inference Group, Cavendish Laboratory,
> University of Cambridge. A zooming text-entry interface in which letters are
> laid out and sized by a language model's probabilities, and the user writes by
> steering continuously into the region of text they want.

`verified: description of the system's operating principle. needs-check — first
publication date, and the URL of MacKay's Google talk, which Don cites on camera
as "a really good Google talk"`

### This is not a resemblance to Shannon. It is Shannon, deliberately.

The connection is **not an analogy noticed afterwards** and must not be
presented as one. MacKay was a professional information theorist — author of
*Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms* (Cambridge University
Press, 2003) — and Dasher is applied information theory by explicit design. The
interface allocates screen area in proportion to predicted probability, which
is the same allocation an arithmetic coder makes when it assigns code length by
probability. **Writing in Dasher is arithmetic decoding driven by a human hand.**

Stated as a symmetry, because this is the part that does the argumentative work:

| | Direction | What the model does | Who supplies the bits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compression | text → bits | predicts the next symbol | the text |
| **Dasher** | **gestures → text** | **predicts the next symbol** | **the person, steering** |

Same predictor, same probabilities, run backwards. The information the person
contributes is exactly the information the model could not predict — which is
Shannon's definition of information, operating as a user interface.

`verified: the arithmetic-coding correspondence and the 2003 book are secure.
needs-check before quoting MacKay on his own intent — get it from his papers or
the talk rather than restating it here.`

**So the Shannon pairing below is a lineage rather than a juxtaposition**, and
saying so is stronger *and* more honest than presenting two independent facts
that happen to rhyme.

**Why it belongs in a file about the parrot slogan.** Dasher is a language model
used as a **prosthesis**, built for people who cannot use a keyboard. The
machinery under it is the same machinery under the insult, and here it produces
no text at all on its own: **every character is chosen by a human, continuously,
with the model doing nothing but making the likely choices easier to reach.**

Don's framing on camera, at
[2:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lL2sw2oYU98&t=126s) and
[3:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lL2sw2oYU98&t=221s):

> "David MacKay's Dasher, which is: writing is navigation in the library of all
> possible books. … This is very much like token prediction."

`verified: ASR transcript of lL2sw2oYU98, lightly repunctuated`

### The inversion, which is the actual counter-metaphor

The slogan's load-bearing word is **stochastic**, not *parrot*, and this is what
answers it. Palm's articulation, read aloud at
[19:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lL2sw2oYU98&t=1167s):

> "The whole idea of infinite monkeys is wrong, because infinite monkeys implies
> infinite randomness. But if I have infinite navigation … every possible text
> exists. I don't need to randomly reach Shakespeare, I can steer there. …
> Faster than infinite random monkeys, because I'm not random. I'm intentional.
> I'm a Dasher user with infinite screens showing infinite possible texts."

`verified: ASR transcript of lL2sw2oYU98. Authored by Palm, a character; see
designs/voicewashing/README.md Case B. Attribute to Palm, not to Don.`

So: **one monkey with infinite typewriters, not infinite monkeys with one
apiece.** The monkeys metaphor assumes the output is sampled; the Dasher
picture has identical underlying statistics and a human steering through them.
Same distribution, opposite epistemic situation, and it is a working piece of
accessibility software rather than a thought experiment.

**Use, and the caution that goes with it.** This is the strongest positive
image in the file, because it does not deny anything the other side says about
the mechanism — it grants the mechanism entirely and relocates the agency.

**Cite it together with Shannon, as one line of work rather than two.**
Prediction measuring a human in 1951; the same mathematics, by an
information theorist who knew exactly whose it was, assisting a human to write
in the 2000s. Neither is a machine talking, and the through-line is documented
rather than rhetorical.

Alan Kay belongs in the same breath and there is a receipt for it — see the
Shannon entry above and
[`../../../designs/NOISY-CHANNEL.md`](../../../designs/NOISY-CHANNEL.md), which
documents Kay praising Shannon's noisy-channel coding theorem *while being
garbled by a noisy channel*, on video, unintentionally.

The caution: it is a *metaphor*, and it describes a mode of use rather than a
proof about what models are. Overclaiming it is the last bullet of Article VII —
"the same overconfident move with the sign reversed." Someone using an LLM
without steering is not covered by the picture, and conceding that first is
cheaper than being told.

## Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, Shmitchell 2021 — what the phrase actually came from

> Bender, E. M., Gebru, T., McMillan-Major, A., Shmitchell, S. (2021). "On the
> Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big? 🦜"
> *FAccT '21*, 610–623.

The definition, which is the part everyone borrows:

> "haphazardly stitching together sequences of linguistic forms it has observed
> in its vast training data, according to probabilistic information about how
> they combine, but without any reference to meaning"

`verified: needs-check — confirm against FAccT '21 proceedings §6`

Fourth author: **Margaret Mitchell, publishing under the pseudonym "Shmargaret
Shmitchell."** Name her both ways or the citation looks careless.

**What the paper principally argues.** It is a position paper about the risks of
ever-larger language models: environmental and financial costs, biases and
hegemonic viewpoints inherited from poorly documented training data, unequal
access and concentration of power, and the danger of people attributing meaning
and accountability to synthetic text.

**And it does make the strong theoretical claim — say so.** Because a language
model is trained on linguistic form with no access to communicative intent, the
paper holds that it cannot possess meaning, understanding, or a model of the
world. **Do not soften this into "the paper does not argue that language models
cannot reason."** It argues something stronger, and conceding the strong version
is what earns the right to the next sentence.

**Where the slogan actually goes wrong**, stated precisely enough to be worth
posting: it discards the paper's detailed analysis of real risks while treating
its most controversial theoretical premise as an established scientific result.
The phrase escaped into pop culture as a drive-by anti-LLM slogan — something
people repeat instead of investigating what these systems represent internally,
how post-training changes their behavior, or what they can actually do.

**The structural irony, worth stating in your own words rather than as a jab:**
the paper's central concern is that fluent text causes people to stop thinking.
Using its title as a conversation-ender is that concern, enacted, by a person.

PDF, which is the link to give since the ACM page is paywalled:
https://s10251.pcdn.co/pdf/2021-bender-parrots.pdf

The prior and more philosophical paper, which is usually what people mean:

> Bender, E. M., Koller, A. (2020). "Climbing towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and
> Understanding in the Age of Data." *ACL 2020*. The octopus thought experiment.

## Lissack 2021 — the objection arrived before the paper did

> Lissack, M. (2021). "The Slodderwetenschap (Sloppy Science) of Stochastic
> Parrots — A Plea for Science to NOT Take the Route Advocated by Gebru and
> Bender." arXiv:2101.10098, January 2021.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10098

> "I find the ethics of the Parrot Paper lacking, and in that lack, I worry
> about the direction in which computer science, machine learning, and
> artificial intelligence are heading. At best, I would describe the
> argumentation and evidentiary practices embodied in the Parrot Paper as
> Slodderwetenschap (Dutch for Sloppy Science) [...] What is missing in the
> Parrot Paper are three critical elements: 1) acknowledgment that it is a
> position paper/advocacy piece rather than research, 2) explicit articulation
> of the critical presuppositions, and 3) explicit consideration of cost/benefit
> trade-offs rather than a mere recitation of potential "harms" as if benefits
> did not matter."

`verified: verbatim from the arXiv abstract`

**Use, and note the date.** January 2021 — a response to the *draft*, before
formal publication, while the author list was still partly unnamed. The
objection that rhetoric was replacing analysis is **as old as the phrase
itself**, which is a fact about the record rather than an opinion about the
paper.

**This is the citation that prevents the reply from reading as pro-industry.**
It is a methodology complaint from inside academia about a position paper being
received as a result, which is the same complaint the corpus makes about the
slogan — one level up. Cite it once and do not lean on it: Lissack's tone is
sharper than anything worth adopting, and quoting the three-element list is
enough.

## Pepperberg — the metaphor does not insult

> Pepperberg, I. M. (1999). *The Alex Studies: Cognitive and Communicative
> Abilities of Grey Parrots.* Harvard University Press.

Roughly thirty years of work with **Alex**, an African grey parrot, on label
learning, categorization by colour, shape and material, and same/different
judgments. Alex died in 2007.

`verified: citation and subject only — no experimental claim quoted here. Get
specific findings from the book or the primary papers before asserting any of
them, and do not repeat the popular-press versions.`

**Use.** Whoever says "stochastic parrot" as an insult is relying on parrots
being mindless, and thirty years of grey-parrot research is the reason that
premise is unsafe. The move is not to claim Alex proves anything about language
models — it does not — but to observe that **the insult depends on an empirical
claim about birds that its user has not checked.** Deployed briefly it is
disarming; pressed hard it becomes its own overreach.

It also explains why the honest version of the cliché refutes itself. Anyone
who says the metaphor works *because* parrots have inner lives has abandoned the
dismissal in the act of making it.

### Why this entry stays thin on purpose

The instinct is to load it up — the label vocabulary, the categorization work,
the same/different judgments, the number comprehension including a zero-like use
of "none." **Resist it, because depth turns this card against you**, in three
ways that all cost more than the card is worth:

1. **It moves the argument to birds.** You will spend the thread on grey parrot
   methodology and single-subject design and Clever Hans controls, and whatever
   you were originally saying is gone. The other person did not come to talk
   about *Psittacus erithacus* and neither did you.
2. **Arguing that parrots are clever concedes the frame.** It accepts that the
   metaphor's force depends on how smart parrots are. The available reply is
   then "fine — so it *is* like a parrot," and you have handed over the
   comparison you were objecting to.
3. **The word doing the work is "stochastic," not "parrot."** The dismissal
   claims *random regurgitation*. Even a maximally clever Alex does not touch
   that claim. [`ARGUMENTS.md`](ARGUMENTS.md) is where the load-bearing answer
   lives, and MacKay's Dasher above is the counter-metaphor.

So the entry is a one-line disarm and a citation, deliberately. **Say that the
insult rests on an unchecked empirical premise, name the book, and move on.**

### The part that is genuinely interesting, and cuts both ways

Worth knowing even though it does not go in a comment. Pepperberg's method was
the **model/rival** technique, adapted from Dietmar Todt: two humans, one
training and one acting as both model and competitor for the trainer's
attention, with roles reversed so the bird observes that the exchange runs in
both directions. Crucially, **the reward was the referential object itself
rather than food** — ask for the cork, get the cork.

`verified: needs-check — the method's structure and the object-as-reward design
are the well-known features of the program; confirm the Todt attribution and
the reversal protocol against the book before describing it in public.`

That design exists to make labels *referential and socially situated* instead of
merely associative. Which is why it is a double-edged citation: **it is evidence
for the grounding argument, which is Bender's actual argument** — that form
acquired without communicative intent is not meaning. Pepperberg went to
enormous lengths to supply exactly the intent and social context that the paper
says large corpora lack.

Hold both. The insult is unearned *and* the serious version of the underlying
worry has real experimental company. Reaching for Pepperberg to win the first
point while pretending the second does not exist is the move this file is
supposed to prevent.

## Kent Pitman on Weizenbaum — the point was how little it takes

> KMP (Kent M. Pitman), on his Lisp implementation of ELIZA, quoted at
> ELIZAGEN, the ELIZA genealogy project.

https://sites.google.com/view/elizagen-org/

> "The thing about the DOCTOR program, while it was quite elaborate, was that it
> sort of missed the point of what Weizenbaum was trying to say. It's not that I
> didn't understand him, but it took me a long time to really come to viscerally
> understand the seriousness of his point. He said that (paraphrasing, maybe
> badly, from memory) long before we got to real AI, we would get to things that
> seemed smart but weren't, and we would overly trust systems that were not
> really smart. He was, for example, very worried about computerized launch of
> nuclear missiles using what was then thought to be AI. But the point of the
> original ELIZA was not to BE elaborate, it was to be simple, and to show how
> even a tiny, tiny program could seem smart. It was hard not to think it could
> be smarter if it were not so tiny, but that misses the point, which was about
> how easily we were confused, not about how much more effectively we could be
> confused if more energy were put in. So whether my DOCTOR or any other was
> making the right point was hard to say. My guess is not."

`verified: verbatim as quoted on HN item 39373567 by xyzzy_plugh, 2024-02-18,
from ELIZAGEN; confirm against the ELIZAGEN page before print use`

**Why this belongs in the canon and not merely in the notes.** It is the
strongest available statement of the concession the corpus is required to make,
and it comes from someone who built one of these programs and then decided his
own elaboration had missed the point.

**The load-bearing sentence: "the point was not to BE elaborate."** Weizenbaum's
alarm was about *how easily people are fooled*, not about how convincingly a
machine could be built to fool them. So **trying to optimize ELIZA misses the
point entirely** — a better ELIZA measures nothing the original did not already
demonstrate.

Pair with the Weizenbaum entries above. Cite this *first*, concede it fully,
and the rest of the argument stops sounding like advocacy.

## Will Wright — the simulator effect, and why it is a tool rather than a verdict

> Wright, W. — "the simulator effect," as recounted in Don Hopkins,
> "Designing User Interfaces to Simulation Games." Written 1996; revised
> circa 2000 after The Sims shipped; revised again 2023 when the lecture video
> was published.

https://donhopkins.medium.com/designing-user-interfaces-to-simulation-games-bd7a9d81e62d

`verified: URL confirmed against Wayback captures from 2022-02-10 onward`

**The article is layered, and the layers are not marked in the text.** At least
three passes are known: the 1996 notes taken at the lecture, a revision around
2000 looking back at what actually shipped in The Sims, and the 2023 revision
accompanying the video's release. **Do not attribute any particular sentence to
a particular year** without checking it against a dated capture. The
reconstruction is tracked in
[`../../../designs/sims/MEDIUM-RESCUE.md`](../../../designs/sims/MEDIUM-RESCUE.md).

Wright's definition, in Don's words rather than Wright's, and it must be
attributed that way:

> Will Wright defined the "Simulator Effect" as how game players imagine a
> simulation is vastly more detailed, deep, rich, and complex than it actually
> is: a magical misunderstanding that you shouldn't talk them out of. He designs
> games to run on two computers at once: the electronic one on the player's
> desk, running his shallow tame simulation, and the biological one in the
> player's head, running their deep wild imagination.

`verified: verbatim from Don's own published account; NOT a Wright quotation —
present it as Don's characterization or not at all`

**Reverse over-engineering** is the corollary: what players and cloners do when
they extrapolate how a game works and drastically overestimate the modeling
behind it, filling gaps with imagination instead of noticing the shortcuts.

The clinical name for the underlying tendency is **apophenia** — Klaus Conrad,
1958, "unmotivated seeing of connections accompanied by a specific feeling of
abnormal meaningfulness."

**Why this is the most useful entry in the file for actually persuading anyone.**
It grants the skeptic's mechanism completely and then declines the verdict.
Over-attribution is real, exploitable and designed-for; Wright built a career on
it. So the effect is **a double-edged tool, like procedural rhetoric** — it
operates whether or not anyone intends it, and the only real question is whether
you are being thoughtful and intentional about it.

That reframing is the whole move: the ELIZA effect and the simulator effect are
the same observation, one treated as an indictment and the other as a craft. A
person who has shipped games knows it is a craft.

**Use.** The answer to "we got it right the first time" and to every argument

## Lanier on Engelbart — the question that reframes the whole argument

> Lanier, J. (2005). "Early Computing's Long, Strange Trip." Review of John
> Markoff, *What the Dormouse Said*. *American Scientist*, July–August 2005.

https://web.archive.org/web/20110312232514/https://www.americanscientist.org/bookshelf/pub/early-computings-long-strange-trip

> "Engelbart once told me a story that illustrates the conflict succinctly. He
> met Marvin Minsky—one of the founders of the field of AI—and Minsky told him
> how the AI lab would create intelligent machines. Engelbart replied, 'You're
> going to do all that for the machines? What are you going to do for the
> people?' This conflict between machine- and human-centered design continues to
> this day."

`verified: verbatim against the Internet Archive capture of the American
Scientist review; first-hand — Engelbart told Lanier directly`

**Link the whole review, not just the quote.** It is short, it is good, and it
reflects on Ted Nelson and the two cultures of computing — human-centered design
at SRI against the AI lab — which is the frame the quote sits in and which does
the work.

**Use, and this is the one to end on.** The question is not rhetorical and it is
not anti-AI. Engelbart is asking what the work is *for*, and it is the right
question to put to a method: a corpus that retrieves human arguments, credits
the humans who made them, and hands a person their own words back is an answer
to it. Doing something for the people, with the machine, is the position — which

## Searle 1980 — what "doesn't really understand" is borrowing

> Searle, J. (1980). "Minds, Brains, and Programs." *Behavioral and Brain
> Sciences* 3(3), 417–457.

`verified: citation only`

**Notes for accurate use.** The Chinese Room argues against a specific
functionalism and was answered in the same issue, including by the Systems
Reply. Searle's own position is that brains cause minds through biological
causal powers — **not** that machine minds are impossible in principle. Anyone
citing him for the stronger claim is citing him for something he did not say.

## Not in the canon, on purpose

- **No quotations about anyone's health, diagnosis, medication or private life**,
  whoever said them and however publicly. They are not part of the argument.
- **No paraphrase presented as quotation.** If it is not in quotation marks with
  a citation, it is orientation, and orientation does not get pasted.
