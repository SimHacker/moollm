# Strict mode

Read this file when the user asks for full ASD-STE100 style ("strict", "100%", "full STE", "compliant"), or when the text is a procedure, a safety instruction or maintenance documentation.

These are paraphrases of the ASD-STE100 Issue 9 rule sections. They are not the official text. For real compliance work, the writer needs the official specification and its dictionary (free from https://www.asd-ste100.org), and training. Never say the result "is STE-compliant". Say "written in STE style".

## 1. Words

- Use only three kinds of words: approved dictionary words, technical nouns and technical verbs.
- Use an approved word only as its approved part of speech and with its approved meaning. Example: "test" and "check" are nouns, not verbs. Write "Do a test of the pump", not "Test the pump". "About" means "concerned with", not "approximately". Use "approximately" for numbers.
- Technical nouns are words that name things in your subject: parts, tools, materials, systems, software, units, documents, colors, damage types, and similar categories. Use the term your field or company approves. Use one term for one item. Do not use a technical noun as a verb ("oil the valve" is wrong, "apply oil to the valve" is correct).
- Technical verbs are verbs for specific processes: manufacturing (drill, solder), computer actions (click, upload, delete) and similar fields. Do not use them as nouns.
- Choose short, easy technical nouns. Do not use slang or regional words.
- Use American English spelling, unless the user's project uses British spelling.

## 2. Multi-word nouns

- A multi-word noun has three words or fewer.
- If a technical name has more than three words, write it in full once. Then use a short form, or hyphenate the words that act as one unit.

## 3. Verbs

- Permitted forms: infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future ("will"), and the past participle as an adjective only ("the installed module", "the module is installed" as a state).
- Do not use perfect tenses ("has opened", "had been"), progressive tenses ("is running"), or modal-plus-passive forms ("can be adjusted", "is to be installed").
- Use "-ing" forms only as part of a technical noun ("logging level") or in titles.
- Use the active voice in procedures. In descriptions, use the passive only if you do not know the actor.
- Put the action in an approved verb, not in a noun: write "install the module", not "do the installation of the module". Exception: when the action word is approved only as a noun ("test", "check"), write "do a test of" or "do a check of".

## 4. Sentences

- Write short, clear sentences.
- Do not leave out words to shorten a sentence. Keep articles, "that" and verbs. No contractions.
- Use a vertical list for complex information.
- Use connecting words to link sentences on related topics.
- Put "the", "a", "an", "this" or "these" before a noun where English needs it.

## 5. Procedures

- A procedural sentence has 20 words or fewer. Warnings and cautions too.
- One instruction per sentence. Two actions in one sentence only if they happen at the same time.
- Write instructions in the imperative ("Remove the cover.").
- If the reader must know a condition first, start with the condition, then a comma, then the command ("If the light comes on, stop the engine.").
- Notes give information only, never instructions. Sentences in notes have 25 words or fewer.

## 6. Descriptions

- Do not use the imperative in descriptive text.
- Give information gradually. One subject per sentence.
- Use key words and phrases to show the logical structure ("first", "then", "as a result").
- Descriptive sentences have 25 words or fewer.
- Use paragraphs to group related information. One topic per paragraph. Six sentences at most per paragraph.

## 7. Safety instructions

- A **WARNING** is for a risk of injury or death. A **CAUTION** is for a risk of damage to equipment, data or property.
- Use the correct signal word for the level of risk.
- Start with a clear, short command or condition.
- Then give the risk or the result. Example:

> **WARNING:** Disconnect the power supply before you remove the cover. The terminals have a voltage that can kill you.

## 8. Punctuation and word count

- All standard punctuation is permitted, except the semicolon.
- Use hyphens to join words that are directly related ("high-pressure pump").
- Parentheses are permitted for references, item numbers, step numbers, abbreviations, singular-and-plural "(s)", short explanations and alternatives.
- Word count:
  - A colon at the end of a list introduction ends the sentence for word count.
  - Text in parentheses counts as one word.
  - A number, a number with its unit, an abbreviation, an alphanumeric identifier, quoted text, a title or label, and a proper noun each count as one word.
  - A hyphenated word counts as one word.

## 9. Writing practices

- If a word-for-word swap does not work, rebuild the sentence.
- Use each approved word correctly.
- Do not make phrasal verbs ("put on" becomes "install" or "attach").
- Keep terms and wording consistent across the whole document.
- Recommendations: keep "that" after verbs like "make sure" and "show". Be careful with "with" (it can mean "by", "has" or "near"). Make pronouns point to one clear noun. Do not use "this" alone. Write "this value", "this step". Avoid Latin abbreviations ("e.g.", "i.e.", "etc."). Write "for example", "that is", "and other". Use inclusive language. Avoid possessives for things ("the cover of the pump", not "the pump's cover").

## 10. Final checklist

Go through this list before you return strict-mode text:

- [ ] No procedural sentence has more than 20 words. No descriptive sentence has more than 25.
- [ ] No paragraph has more than six sentences or more than one topic.
- [ ] Every instruction is imperative, active and has one action.
- [ ] No perfect, progressive or modal-passive verb forms.
- [ ] No "-ing" verb forms outside technical nouns.
- [ ] No semicolons. No contractions. No Latin abbreviations.
- [ ] No noun stack longer than three words.
- [ ] "Check" and "test" are used as nouns only.
- [ ] Modal verbs are only "can", "must" and "will".
- [ ] Each thing has one name across the whole text.
- [ ] Warnings and cautions come before the step they protect.
- [ ] Every fact, number and identifier from the source is still there, at the same strength.
