# Poisoned Well

**Class:** method (cautionary) · **Attribution:** the screen-character
disaster of 1997–2004, as a general pattern

> **Before entering a design space, find out who poisoned it — and study
> exactly how. A good principle shipped obnoxiously can make its whole space
> radioactive for decades.**

The founding case: Clifford Nass's research said people respond socially to
computers, so social interfaces should work. Microsoft licensed the insight
and shipped it as **Microsoft Agent** — Clippy, Bob's descendants, and the
runtime that BonziBuddy rode in on. The deployment fused a correct principle
(social presence) to fatal execution: **interruption without invitation,
impersonation without competence, and no way to say never again**. Screen
characters became a global punchline, the whole space went radioactive, and
serious work on embodied agents lost its funding, its dignity, and roughly
twenty-five years — until the LLM era made characters worth trusting again.
The final insult: the poisoning discredited Nass's research in the public
mind, when the research had actually predicted the failure — a social actor
that behaves like a rude colleague gets treated like one.

The method, when entering any space with a smoking crater in it:

1. **Name the poisoner** — find the artifact that made the space a punchline.
2. **Autopsy the failure precisely** — separate the principle (usually sound)
   from the execution (usually the poison). Clippy's crime was etiquette, not
   embodiment.
3. **Steer clear of the infamous moves by name** — they explored the worst
   ways so you don't have to; your design review should be able to say "this
   is the part where Clippy interrupted."
4. **Check whether the good path was killed rather than disproven** — Free
   The Sims did screen characters right and died of ActiveX and permissions,
   not of user rejection
   ([demo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Nzz0cFSmgiM)); a poisoned well
   often has an unpoisoned spring next to it.

Other craters worth the same autopsy: Newton handwriting recognition poisoned
pen computing (a Doonesbury strip did lasting damage to a fixable beta);
pop-ups poisoned an entire generation of web advertising formats; 90s VR
sickness froze that field for two decades.
