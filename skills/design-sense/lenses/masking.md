# Masking

**Class:** lens · **Attribution:** Scott McCloud (*Understanding Comics*, 1993, ch. 2)

> **Abstract character, detailed world.**
>
> **You can trade resolution for identification.** That is the essence of *Understanding
> Comics*, and it is an exchange rate, not a compromise.

Resolution is spendable. Detail subtracted from a character is not detail lost — it is bought
identification, because every specific a face carries is a specific that can contradict the
viewer. A photorealistic face is somebody else's; a six-pixel face is yours. The knob runs
both ways, which is why the trade is a design decision each time and not a style.

The image for it: **lowering the resolution is like taking the batteries out of a talking doll.**
A doll that talks says the same sentence forever in someone else's voice, and every time it
speaks it is not the child speaking. Take the batteries out and it says whatever the child needs
it to say this afternoon. Simlish is that removal done deliberately in the audio channel — tone
with no words, the listener supplies the line — and pixel art is the same removal in the visual
one.

Identification is what McCloud was buying. Two more things come off the same purchase, and
they are why this lens keeps showing up in content economies rather than only in comics:

**Participation.** Low resolution lowers the authoring floor. A player can draw a 16×16 sprite
or recolour a tile in an afternoon with free tools; nobody makes a photoreal asset without a
pipeline, a suite, and a skill set. Every increment of fidelity you demand of your own art you
also demand of anyone who wants to add to it, which quietly decides whether the game has a
modding community or an asset store.

**Portability.** Abstract assets compose across styles. A low-res chair fits almost any world;
a photoreal chair clashes with everything not lit the same way. So abstraction is also what
lets content — and characters — travel between games at all, which is the precondition the
[soul-city](../../soul-city/README.md) bridges depend on. That consequence is ours, not
McCloud's.

The masking effect: readers identify with an abstract, iconic face — it's a mask
they wear — while a detailed environment supplies believability. Simplify the
character and the audience projects themselves in; detail the world and the
projection has somewhere real to live. Detail the character instead and you get a
specific *other person*, breaking identification (and inviting the uncanny valley).

The exhibit ladder, running in public across product cycles:

- **The Sims 1 is masking in 3D.** Stylized cartoon faces, low-poly bodies,
  exaggerated walks, and Simlish — deliberately abstract pseudo-language with only
  emotional tone, the listener supplies the words — living in detailed isometric
  houses with real-time shadows and near-photoreal furniture. A dollhouse built on
  McCloud's principle.
- **Tiny Life spends the resolution on purpose, and says so in its title.** Pixel people in a
  detailed town, made by one developer — small in the same direction Little Computer People was
  ([sims-tiny-life.md](../../../designs/sims/sims-tiny-life.md#the-masking-principle)).
- **Miis are masking as an avatar system** — and Tomodachi Life's toon-Miis
  succeeded where Switch Sports's detailed Sportsmates failed: same publisher,
  same principle, opposite choices, predictable outcomes.
